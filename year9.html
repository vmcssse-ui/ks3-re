Unit 1 · Weeks 1–8 · 8 lessons

Humanism and Society

Marriage as a civil right, sexual relationships, family planning and equality — Humanist and Christian views side by side.

▤Unit booklet❐Unit folder▤Unit cover (Publisher)
1Week

Marriage — Civil Rights

Lesson #w1

In the UK marriage is a legal contract before it is a religious one. Civil ceremonies carry no religious content, and since 2014 same-sex couples have been able to marry. Humanists hold their own ceremonies, built on the couple's promises rather than on God.

Note First lesson of the year — the expectations deck runs before the content.

▶Lesson deck▶Expectations deck
2Week

Sexual Relationships

Lesson #w2

What sex is for, and who decides. Christian teaching gives it two purposes — creating a family, and uniting a couple — and places it inside marriage. Humanists judge by consent, honesty and whether anyone is harmed.

▶Lesson deck▤Bell work — crossword
3Week

Family Planning

Lesson #w3

Choosing when, and whether, to have children. The Roman Catholic Church allows only natural methods, teaching that every act must stay open to life. Most Protestants and Humanists accept contraception as responsible parenthood.

5Week

Equality

Lesson #w5

Everyone is of equal worth — for Christians because all are made in the image of God, for Humanists because we share one humanity. The Equality Act 2010 turns that principle into law.

▶Lesson deck
6Week

Marriage and the Sacred + D type

Lesson #w6

Why marriage is holy for Christians: a sacrament and a covenant made before God, not only a contract. The second deck teaches the structure of a 12-mark answer, and is worth coming back to all year.

Note The D-type structure deck is the one to reuse before every 12-mark question.

Sexual Relationships in Christianity

Lesson #w7

Denominations disagree. Catholics tie sex to a sacramental marriage open to life; the Church of England accepts contraception and, for many members, faithful same-sex relationships; Quakers judge by love and faithfulness.

12Week

Divorce and Remarriage

Lesson #w12

Mark 10:9 says what God has joined together must not be separated. Catholics allow an annulment but not a divorce; Anglicans and most Protestants allow divorce and, in some cases, remarriage in church.

▶Lesson deck
13Week

Equality of Men and Women in the Home

Lesson #w13

Ephesians has been read as giving the husband headship and the wife a subordinate role. Most Christians today, and Humanists, argue instead for equal partnership — shared money, shared parenting, shared decisions.

Gender Prejudice and Discrimination

Lesson #w14

Prejudice is the attitude, discrimination the act. Unequal pay, the suffragettes who won the vote by protest, and the churches that do and do not ordain women.

16Week

Crime

Lesson #w16

What crime is, and why people commit it. Humanists look at the causes — poverty, addiction, poor education — and argue that reducing those prevents more crime than punishment alone. The justice deck sets out the vocabulary.

▶Lesson deck▶Justice deck▤Bell work — definitions
17Week

Attitudes to Human Rights

Lesson #w17

Rights you hold simply for being human, set out in the 1948 Universal Declaration and defended by the United Nations. Freedom of expression, the right to a fair trial, and what happens when two rights collide.

▶Lesson deck▤Worksheet — human rights
18Week

Attitudes to Punishment

Lesson #w18

The five aims of punishment — protection, retribution, deterrence, reformation and reparation. Which one a society puts first tells you what it thinks punishment is actually for.

▶Lesson deck

20Week

Restorative Justice

Lesson #w20

Offender and victim meet, so the offender hears the harm they caused and agrees how to put it right. Lower reoffending, and victims who feel heard — but it does not suit every crime.

▶Lesson deck▤Bell work — definitions
21Week

Moral and Natural Evil

Lesson #w21

Moral evil comes from human choice; natural evil comes from the world itself. Both raise the same question: how could a good, all-powerful God allow this much suffering?

23Week

Islam and Justice

Lesson #w23

Allah is both perfectly just and endlessly merciful. Muslims are told to stand firm for justice even when it goes against themselves, and Shari'ah sets out what that means in law.

▶Lesson deck▤Bell work
24Week

Islam and Crime

Lesson #w24

What Islam counts as crime and why — offences against God, against other people and against property — with the harm done to the whole ummah kept in view.

▶Lesson deck
25Week

The Qur'an and the Hadd

Lesson #w25

Hadd offences carry fixed punishments named in the Qur'an. The standards of evidence required are extremely high, which is why many Muslims argue they should almost never apply in practice.

▶Lesson deck

Unit 5 · Weeks 30–33 · 4 lessons

Islam and Forgiveness

Treatment of criminals, capital punishment, forgiveness, and the question of God.

▤Unit booklet❐Unit folder
30Week

Treatment of Criminals

Lesson #w30

How prisoners are treated is a test of a society's values — conditions, a fair trial, legal representation. Islam requires punishment to be just, and never cruel.

▶Lesson deck
31Week

Capital Punishment

Lesson #w31

The Qur'an permits the death penalty for murder, but praises those who forgive instead. Set that against wrongful convictions, the sanctity of life, and the evidence on whether it deters.

▶Lesson deck
32Week

Forgiveness

Lesson #w32

Giving up the right to revenge. Allah is described as the Most Merciful; Jesus forgave from the cross. Forgiving someone is not the same as cancelling their punishment.

▶Lesson deck
33Week

The Question of God

Lesson #w33

Does God exist, and what would count as evidence either way? Arguments from design and from cause, set against the problem of suffering — the bridge into GCSE.

▶Lesson deck
